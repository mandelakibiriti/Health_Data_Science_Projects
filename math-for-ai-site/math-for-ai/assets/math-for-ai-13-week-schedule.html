Thirteen weeks of mathematics for building AI systems

A reading-and-building schedule, 7 September to 6 December 2026, drawn from four Schaum's Outlines: Linear Algebra, Calculus, Statistics, and Advanced Mathematics for Engineers and Scientists. Each week pairs specific chapters with the AI concepts they unlock, from classical machine learning through transformers and diffusion models.

Weeks 1–4: The substrateVectors, matrices, linear maps, calculus refresh. Everything a neural network computes is built from these.
Weeks 5–8: Learning from dataProbability, distributions, estimation, testing, regression, and the chain rule that becomes backpropagation.
Weeks 9–12: Structure and dynamicsGradients and optimisation, eigenstructure and SVD, Fourier and vector calculus, differential equations. The mathematics of deep learning and generative models.
Week 13: ConsolidationCapstone build and a written map from each chapter to where it lives in a modern AI stack.

How to use the four books

Schaum's Outlines are not meant to be read cover to cover. Each chapter opens with a compressed theory summary, then earns its keep through solved problems. The method that works: read the summary once, work three to five solved problems with the solution covered, then attempt five to eight supplementary problems. Roughly 40% of the total page count across the four books is scheduled below; the rest (curve sketching, related rates, volumes of revolution, Bessel and Legendre functions, process control) is either prerequisite refresher or engineering material with little bearing on AI.

Linear Algebra (4th ed., 13 ch.)The most important of the four. Nine of thirteen chapters scheduled.
Calculus (5th ed., 59 ch.)Short chapters. Used selectively: derivatives, exp/log, integration basics, series, partial derivatives, gradients, multiple integrals.
Statistics (18 ch.)Chapters 3–14 are the core. Descriptive statistics through probability, distributions, estimation, testing and least squares.
Advanced Mathematics for Engineers & Scientists (16 ch.)Dipped into for vector analysis, Fourier, ODEs, special functions, and the calculus of variations.

Priority labels on each reading: Core = do the solved problems; Skim = read the theory summary only; Optional = if time allows.

Phase 1 · The substrate (Weeks 1–4)

17–13 Sep

Vectors, dot products, and the algebra of matrices

Read

  • LinAlgCh 1 Vectors in Rⁿ (1.1–1.6) Core; 1.7–1.8 complex vectors Skim
  • LinAlgCh 2 Algebra of Matrices (2.1–2.10, 2.12 block matrices) Core
  • CalcCh 6 Functions, Ch 7 Limits, Ch 8 Continuity Skim
  • CalcCh 9 The Derivative, Ch 10 Differentiation Rules Core, as refresher

Where it lives in AI

A tensor is a stack of matrices; every dense layer, every attention score, every embedding lookup is matrix multiplication. The dot product is similarity, which is exactly what a vector database computes when it ranks retrieved passages for a RAG system. Block matrices are how multi-head attention concatenates heads and how weight matrices are sharded across GPUs. Summation notation (2.4) is the notation you will read in every paper.

Build

Implement matrix multiplication with explicit loops, then with np.einsum, and confirm they agree. Write cosine_similarity(a, b) and rank a handful of hand-made "embedding" vectors against a query.

214–20 Sep

Solving systems, and the idea of a vector space

Read

  • LinAlgCh 3 Systems of Linear Equations (3.1–3.11 Gaussian elimination, echelon form; 3.13 LU decomposition) Core
  • LinAlgCh 4 Vector Spaces (4.2–4.9: span, subspaces, independence, basis, dimension, rank) Core; 4.10–4.11 Skim
  • CalcCh 11 Implicit Differentiation, Ch 13 Mean Value Theorem, Ch 14 Maxima and Minima Core

Where it lives in AI

Rank is the single most useful idea this week. LoRA fine-tunes a large model by learning a low-rank update W + BA instead of a full matrix, and that only makes sense once rank and dimension are intuitive. Linear independence explains why collinear features break linear regression and why embedding dimensions can be "wasted". Basis and coordinates are what a tokenizer plus embedding table actually produce: a coordinate vector in a learned basis. Maxima and minima set up optimisation.

Build

Solve a small linear system by hand with row reduction, then check with np.linalg.solve. Generate a random 512×512 matrix as B @ A with rank 8, confirm np.linalg.matrix_rank, and count how many parameters that saves versus the full matrix.

321–27 Sep

Linear maps, change of basis, and exp/log

Read

  • LinAlgCh 5 Linear Mappings (5.2–5.6: kernel, image, isomorphisms) Core
  • LinAlgCh 6 Linear Mappings and Matrices (6.2–6.4: matrix representation, change of basis, similarity) Core
  • CalcCh 25 Natural Logarithm, Ch 26 Exponential and Logarithmic Functions Core
  • CalcCh 27 L'Hôpital's Rule, Ch 28 Exponential Growth and Decay Skim

Where it lives in AI

A neural network layer is a linear map followed by a pointwise non-linearity; the kernel of that map is information the layer throws away. Change of basis is what a projection head does, what PCA does, and what "rotating" an embedding space means in interpretability work. The exponential and logarithm underpin softmax, log-likelihood, cross-entropy, and the log-sum-exp trick that keeps training numerically stable. Exponential decay is your learning-rate schedule.

Build

Apply a 2×2 matrix to a grid of points and plot before/after to see a linear map as a geometric deformation. Implement softmax naively, watch it overflow on large logits, then fix it with the log-sum-exp shift.

428 Sep – 4 Oct

Inner products, norms, orthogonality, determinants, integrals

Read

  • LinAlgCh 7 Inner Product Spaces (7.2–7.8 Cauchy–Schwarz, orthogonal bases, Gram–Schmidt, positive definite matrices; 7.10 normed spaces) Core
  • LinAlgCh 8 Determinants (8.1–8.10, 8.13 determinants and volume) Core; rest Optional
  • CalcCh 22 Antiderivatives, Ch 23 Definite Integral, Ch 24 Fundamental Theorem Core

Where it lives in AI

Norms are everywhere: L2 weight decay, L1 sparsity, gradient clipping, LayerNorm and RMSNorm, and the unit-normalised embeddings that make cosine similarity a plain dot product. Cauchy–Schwarz is why cosine similarity lies in [−1, 1]. Orthogonal matrices preserve norms, which is why orthogonal initialisation and QR-based tricks help deep networks train. The determinant as a volume factor is the key to normalising flows and change-of-variables in generative models. Integrals are expectations, and expectations are what every loss function really is.

Build

Implement Gram–Schmidt and verify Qᵀ Q = I. Implement LayerNorm from the formula. Compute the determinant of a random 2×2 and measure how it scales the area of a unit square.

Phase 1 checkpoint. You should be able to explain, without notes: what rank means for LoRA, why softmax needs the log-sum-exp shift, and what LayerNorm computes and why.

Phase 2 · Learning from data (Weeks 5–8)

55–11 Oct

Describing data, and probability from first principles

Read

  • StatsCh 1 Variables and Graphs, Ch 2 Frequency Distributions Skim
  • StatsCh 3 Mean, Median, Mode; Ch 4 Standard Deviation and Dispersion Core
  • StatsCh 5 Moments, Skewness, Kurtosis Core
  • StatsCh 6 Elementary Probability Theory (conditional probability, independence, Bayes, random variables, expectation, combinatorics) Core, the heaviest week in this book

Where it lives in AI

Standardisation (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation) is the first thing done to features and is exactly what BatchNorm computes on activations. Moments are the language of distribution shift, and skew and kurtosis tell you when a Gaussian assumption is lying. Bayes' theorem is naive Bayes, Bayesian optimisation for hyperparameters, the posterior in a VAE, and the correct way to reason about a model's predicted probabilities against a base rate. Expectation is the operator inside every loss: training minimises expected loss over the data distribution.

Build

Take any tabular dataset, compute mean, std, skew, and kurtosis per column, and standardise it. Implement Bayes' rule for a diagnostic-test style problem (prior, sensitivity, specificity) and plot posterior against prior.

612–18 Oct

Distributions, sampling, and the central limit theorem

Read

  • StatsCh 7 Binomial, Normal, and Poisson Distributions Core
  • StatsCh 8 Elementary Sampling Theory (sampling distributions, standard error, CLT) Core
  • CalcCh 35 Improper Integrals Core, needed for the normal density
  • CalcCh 42 Sequences, Ch 43 Infinite Series Skim

Where it lives in AI

The Bernoulli/binomial is a sigmoid output and binary cross-entropy. The Gaussian is weight initialisation, the noise added at each step of a diffusion model, the latent prior of a VAE, and the assumption behind mean-squared-error loss. Poisson models counts (tokens per document, events per hour). The central limit theorem is why a minibatch gradient is approximately the true gradient plus Gaussian noise, which is the whole justification for stochastic gradient descent and the reason batch size trades off against noise.

Build

Draw samples from a skewed distribution, average them in batches of 1, 8, 64, 512, and watch the histogram of batch means turn Gaussian with standard error shrinking as 1/√n. Overlay the normal density you integrated by hand.

719–25 Oct

Estimation, decisions, and testing whether a model is better

Read

  • StatsCh 9 Statistical Estimation Theory (unbiased and efficient estimators, confidence intervals) Core
  • StatsCh 10 Statistical Decision Theory (hypotheses, Type I/II errors, power, p-values) Core
  • StatsCh 11 Small Sampling Theory (t and chi-square distributions) Core
  • StatsCh 12 The Chi-Square Test Skim

Where it lives in AI

Maximum likelihood estimation is model training: minimising negative log-likelihood is the same act as fitting parameters, and cross-entropy loss is MLE for a categorical output. Confidence intervals are what an honest evaluation reports on accuracy or win-rate; a 0.4-point gain on a 500-example eval is usually noise. Type I and II errors map directly to false positives and negatives and to the threshold you set on a classifier. Chi-square tests check whether a feature and a label are independent, and whether an LLM's output categories differ between two prompts.

Build

Bootstrap a 95% confidence interval for the accuracy of a classifier on a held-out set. Simulate two models with true accuracies 0.80 and 0.82 on 500 examples and count how often a paired test can tell them apart.

826 Oct – 1 Nov

Least squares, correlation, and the multivariable chain rule

Read

  • StatsCh 13 Curve Fitting and Least Squares Core
  • StatsCh 14 Correlation Theory Core; Ch 15 Multiple and Partial Correlation Skim
  • CalcCh 48 Partial Derivatives Core
  • CalcCh 49 Total Differential, Differentiability, Chain Rules Core, read twice
  • CalcCh 47 Taylor and Maclaurin Series Core

Where it lives in AI

Linear regression by least squares is the first machine-learning model and the normal equations tie it back to Week 2. Correlation versus causation is the discipline behind feature selection and behind reading any evaluation. The multivariable chain rule is backpropagation: automatic differentiation is a machine that applies Ch 49 across a computation graph. The total differential is a first-order Taylor expansion, which is what gradient descent assumes about the loss near the current weights.

Build

Fit linear regression three ways: the normal equations, gradient descent with hand-derived partial derivatives, and np.polyfit. Then differentiate a small two-layer network by hand using the chain rule and check against finite differences.

Phase 2 checkpoint. You should be able to derive cross-entropy loss from maximum likelihood, explain why SGD works from the CLT, and compute gradients of a two-layer network by hand.

Phase 3 · Structure and dynamics (Weeks 9–12)

92–8 Nov

Gradients, curvature, and constrained optimisation

Read

  • CalcCh 50 Space Vectors, Ch 52 Directional Derivatives and Extrema (gradient, Hessian test, Lagrange multipliers) Core
  • CalcCh 21 Differentials and Newton's Method Core
  • AdvMathCh 16 Calculus of Variations (Euler's equation, constraints, Lagrange multipliers) Skim theory; Optional problems

Where it lives in AI

The gradient points uphill, so the negative gradient is the descent direction; the directional derivative tells you how much a step in any direction changes the loss. The Hessian describes curvature: sharp versus flat minima, why Adam rescales per-parameter, and why second-order methods are expensive. Newton's method is the ancestor of every quasi-Newton optimiser. Lagrange multipliers turn constrained problems into unconstrained ones, which is how SVMs are derived and how a KL penalty in PPO or RLHF keeps a fine-tuned policy close to its base model.

Build

Write gradient descent with numerical gradients on the Rosenbrock function and plot the path. Add momentum and see the difference. Solve "maximise xy subject to x + y = 10" with a Lagrange multiplier and by substitution.

109–15 Nov

Eigenvalues, the spectral theorem, and singular values

Read

  • LinAlgCh 9 Diagonalization: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors (9.1–9.6) Core, the most important chapter in the four books
  • LinAlgCh 12 Bilinear, Quadratic, Hermitian Forms (12.5–12.6 quadratic forms, law of inertia) Core
  • LinAlgCh 13 Linear Operators on Inner Product Spaces (13.4–13.10: self-adjoint, orthogonal/unitary, positive definite, spectral theorem) Core
  • AdvMathCh 15 Matrices (eigenvalue theorems) Skim, as a second angle
  • LinAlgCh 10 Canonical Forms, Ch 11 Dual Space Optional

Where it lives in AI

PCA is the eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix. Applying the spectral theorem to AᵀA gives the singular value decomposition, which none of the four books names but which you can now derive; SVD is low-rank approximation, model compression, the initialisation used by some LoRA variants, and the way to read what an attention head "does". Eigenvalues of the Hessian are the curvature you met last week. The spectral norm bounds how much a layer can stretch an input, which is Lipschitz control in GANs and robustness work. Eigenvectors of a transition matrix give PageRank and the stationary distribution of a Markov chain; eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian are what graph neural networks and spectral clustering operate on.

Build

Implement PCA via np.linalg.eigh on the covariance matrix and via SVD of the centred data; confirm they match. Compress a grayscale image by keeping the top-k singular values and plot error against k. Implement power iteration and recover the dominant eigenvector.

1116–22 Nov

Multiple integrals, vector calculus, and Fourier

Read

  • CalcCh 54 Double and Iterated Integrals, Ch 57 Triple Integrals Core theory, light on problems
  • AdvMathCh 5 Vector Analysis (gradient, divergence, Jacobian, change of variables) Core
  • AdvMathCh 6 Multiple, Line and Surface Integrals Skim
  • AdvMathCh 7 Fourier Series Core; Ch 8 Fourier Integrals Skim

Where it lives in AI

Multiple integrals are marginalisation: p(x) = ∫ p(x, z) dz is the intractable quantity that VAEs bound with the ELBO and that diffusion models sidestep. The Jacobian and change-of-variables formula are the entire mechanism of normalising flows and the reason the determinant reappears. Fourier series explain sinusoidal positional encodings and RoPE in transformers, why a convolution is a multiplication in frequency space, why networks learn low frequencies first, and what a spectrogram is before an audio model sees it.

Build

Generate the sinusoidal positional-encoding matrix from the transformer paper's formula and plot it as a heatmap; check that nearby positions have higher dot products. Verify the convolution theorem with np.fft on a 1-D signal. Monte-Carlo estimate a double integral and compare with the iterated integral.

1223–29 Nov

Differential equations and the dynamics of generative models

Read

  • AdvMathCh 2 Ordinary Differential Equations (first-order, separable, linear) Core
  • AdvMathCh 3 Linear Differential Equations Skim
  • CalcCh 59 Differential Equations of First and Second Order Core, as the gentler version
  • AdvMathCh 9 Gamma, Beta and Other Special Functions Core theory
  • AdvMathCh 4 Laplace Transforms, Ch 12 Partial Differential Equations Optional

Where it lives in AI

Gradient descent with a small step size is Euler's method on the ODE dw/dt = −∇L, and learning-rate schedules are its time-stepping. Neural ODEs make that literal. Diffusion models add Gaussian noise via a first-order linear stochastic differential equation and generate by integrating it backwards; the sampler you choose (DDIM, Euler, Heun) is an ODE solver from this chapter. Exponential moving averages of weights and Adam's moment estimates are discretised first-order linear ODEs. The gamma and beta functions define the Beta and Dirichlet distributions used as priors in Bayesian models and in topic modelling.

Build

Simulate the forward diffusion process on 1-D data with the closed-form noising formula and watch the distribution become standard normal. Implement Euler and Heun steps on a simple ODE and compare error. Plot the Beta distribution for several parameter pairs using the beta function.

Week 13 · Consolidation

1330 Nov – 6 Dec

Capstone build and the written map

Do

  • Mon–Tue: re-work the supplementary problems you got wrong or skipped in Weeks 4, 8 and 10 (the three checkpoint weeks).
  • Wed–Fri: the capstone build, below.
  • Sat: write a two-page document in your own words: for each chapter you studied, one line on where it appears in a modern AI stack. This is the artefact that will still be useful in a year.
  • Sun: decide what to fill next from the gap list.

Capstone build (NumPy only)

A single-head self-attention block from scratch: token embeddings plus sinusoidal positions (Wk 1, 11), Q/K/V projections (Wk 3), scaled dot-product scores (Wk 1, 4), softmax with log-sum-exp (Wk 3), a residual connection and LayerNorm (Wk 4), and a cross-entropy loss with hand-derived gradients for the output layer (Wk 7, 8), trained with SGD plus momentum (Wk 6, 9) on a toy next-token task. Then reduce the attention weight matrices with SVD (Wk 10) and measure how much rank you can remove before loss degrades.

What these four books do not cover

The Schaum's set is strong on the classical core and silent on several things a modern AI engineer needs. Plan to fill these from other sources after Week 13 (the free textbook Mathematics for Machine Learning by Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong covers most of them, as do the Stanford CS229 lecture notes).

Information theoryEntropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, mutual information. The loss functions of language models and the objective of RLHF are written in this language.
Singular value decomposition, namedDerivable from Week 10 but never stated as its own topic. Worth a dedicated treatment.
Multivariate GaussianCovariance matrices, Mahalanobis distance, conditioning and marginalising. Gaussian processes, Kalman filters, and the maths inside diffusion.
Markov chains and MDPsStationary distributions, Bellman equations, policy gradients. Needed for RL, RLHF and agentic decision-making.
Matrix calculus notationJacobians of matrix-valued functions and the layout conventions used in papers and autodiff.
Convex optimisationConvex sets and functions, duality, KKT conditions. Explains when a training problem has a unique answer.
Numerical stabilityFloating point, conditioning, mixed precision. Why fp16 training diverges and bf16 does not.
Measure-theoretic probabilityOptional. Only needed for reading theory papers on SDEs and score matching rigorously.
